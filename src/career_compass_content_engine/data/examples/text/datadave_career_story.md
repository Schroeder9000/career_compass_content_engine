# Navigating Career Pivots without Starting Over

My career story begins in high school, when I was first trying to select a career path. At the time, 2001, I was very interested in AI and robotics, but those fields were very early in development, so I was ushered into a computer engineering major.

But before college even started, I got a chance opportunity to audition for a very competitive acting program (at a different college) and I was offered a spot. Theater and acting had been another passion of mine, so I took the offer and changed colleges two weeks before classes began, switching from computer engineering to acting. In retrospect, this was not a great "career" move, but I'll come back to that later.

Four years of undergrad led me to a graduate acting program at NYU, which took another 3 years, and then I graduated, ready to pursue my dream of being a professional actor (after only 7 years of higher education and training). An agency signed me, and I landed leading roles in off-off-broadway shows and an independent film. Unfortunately, the year I graduated was 2008 and just 6 months after joining the working world, the housing market collapsed and the subsequent financial recession made it a terrible time to be starting a career, any career, let alone a career in acting or the arts.

I stayed in New York and continued acting and bartending, but discovered an interest in business. Especially the idea of "working on your business, not in your business." The concept of systems design was very appealing to me, so I launched a small film company with 2 friends that would produce our own creative work, develop new marketing products for actors (video headshots), and other commercial work. While the company didn't take off, I used that experience to land a full time job as a video/film producer for an international disaster relief, non-profit organization.

That was my first full-time job, and while it didn't pay much, it was a big step up from bartending. In those previous years I had been a bartender, doorman, taxi cab driver, office temp, carpenter and just about any other (legal) job that I could do to make money and keep my financial head above water. So landing a full-time salary (with health insurance!!!) was a game-changer. I thought I had hit the jackpot! hahaha

The job was mostly a good one and allowed me to use my creative storytelling skills, learn video production skills, and travel all over the world (Mexico, Honduras, Puerto Rico, Philippines, North Korea, Siberia, Uganda, Cameroon) while telling the important stories of people affected by disease, famine, natural disasters, warfare and worse. I also co-wrote and produced a feature documentary film about the Ebola epidemic in 2014, which opened in over 700 theaters around the country, won a number of film festival awards, then went to streaming. It was definitely a job that was high on experience, if low on compensation.

During those early career years, I also met and married my wife, so it was a happy and busy time. But I was anxious to grow my career and didn't see a path forward. I thought I had a good degree from NYU, but also thought that I needed to build some business acumen. So I pursued an online Master's degree in Marketing at Northwestern University. Online master's degree programs were relatively new at the time, but it allowed me to continue to work and pay for tuition out of pocket. This was possibly a misstep in my professional development. I thought that I needed a new degree to make a career change or find a new job and that was a mistake. The Northwestern credential on my resume may have opened a few doors, but that is hard to tell. But it was the beginning of my pursuit of online education, which has been hugely beneficial to me over the years.

The first big career risk I took was leaving my first professional job to join a small 8-person non-profit as a leader of the communications and marketing of the organization. I had big hopes and high ambitions, but it ended up being a personal and professional disaster. More than likely, I was not ready for a full leadership role, I didn't have the resources that I need to accomplish my goals and I didn't have enough experience in marketing to be effective. I ended up managing interns, treading water and eventually burning out so hard that I had to resign and take a month off of work. I learned some important lessons that going into a leadership role too early can ultimately damage your long-term success, and if you don't take care of your self personally, then the inevitable crash from burnout will be much worse than taking a pause to rest and recover. This was one of the most painful and difficult career lessons that I went through, costing me credibility, confidence, reputation and time.

My next job was to jump into a general marketing role at a yoga and meditation retreat center. This was more of a job out of necessity, because it was the best option available at the time, but it was a good job where I was able to use my skills and time to benefit the organization and stakeholders. During this time I finished my master's degree at Northwestern and worked on finding a new job. My wife and I decided it was time to move back to Atlanta, where she grew up, and I focused on finding a job in the tech sector in Atlanta. It was a long, gruelling job search lasting 7 months, but I finally secured an offer at a great martech company named CallRail that was going from startup to scale up. The offer was a good one with a strong salary and I would be managing a website optimization program, so it was exciting new work in an interesting field. My wife and I packed up and moved to Atlanta to begin our new life.

Working in tech brought a number of culture changes for me. This is when I discovered that despite all of my education and previous professional experience, I was going to have to learn the ropes all over again. Most of my graduate studies had focused on high-level concepts like consumer insight or brand management. But now the tools that I needed the most were digital marketing tools like SEO, Paid Advertising, web development, CRM, etc. I also needed to learn and acclimate to tech culture. 

I was very successful in the role early on and when my direct manager left, I was received an unofficial battlefield promotion to manage the Demand Generation team, my former team mates and colleagues. This was a tough assignment and I struggled to build relationships with stakeholders and make meaningful progress. What I found is that I was not naturally equipped to be a manager and often preferred to focus on my own work and professional learning journey. I was also interest in growing my expertise in analytics, so when a new VP of Marketing arrived, I managed to negotiate a transition for me into a marketing analytics role, a first for the organization and myself. This work was relatively good and helped me to push my expertise forward, but I didn't have any analytics mentors or co-workers that I could learn from, so I was somewhat isolated in my role.

Soon I managed to secure an offer with a Bay-area start up focused on AI Website Optimization. It was a general marketing role focused on demand gen and analytics, on a small 5-person marketing team. One of the chief reasons I accepted the role was to work with the VP of Marketing, who I saw as a potential mentor. He was an experienced leader with an MBA from Harvard and a long track record of working in the industry. The role was good at first, but three months in the VP of Marketing was dismissed by senior leadership and I found myself managing much of his work while reporting to the CEO, including launching a new website, managing a brand overhaul and deploying a $2M/year ad budget. In addition, my wife and I were welcoming our first child, navigating the unexpected death of her father and dealing with some significant health issues; so it was also a very personally stressful time. But, thanks to some of my past failures and learnings, I had developed some new resilience and was able to effectively navigate the situation.

But when the new marketing leader arrived, it was soon apparent that it was going to be time to find a new opportunity and I wanted to go deeper into the world of analytics. The job market was strong so I found a new role quickly at my first publicly traded company, Upwork, a two-sided marketplace for freelancer talent. I was the new Lead Analyst for Enterprise Marketing, a new role and function. In this role I would build out much of my technical expertise in analytics, as well as finish a certification in data science. I was able to leverage these tools to build an entire reporting framework and suite of dashboards for the marketing team. I also managed to use my new data science skills to drive important analyses and build predictive models that were deployed into production. I was very successful at the work, but again struggled to manage stakeholder relationships. This was my first real "corporate" job and the management structure at this company was extremely aggressive, boarding on toxic. But I managed to keep my head on straight and navigate the ups and downs of the organization until they eventually laid of over 20% of the workforce including me.

I had been building my LinkedIn profile and presence for years to prepare for this eventuality, so I was prepared and flipped on my LinkedIn "Open to Work" notification the day after the layoff and a recruiter reached out to me that very afternoon. After a 2-3 week interview process, I was offered a new role and accepted just 28 days after I was laid off. This was once case where I could see that my investment in career development, in positioning myself and in building a network paid off big time.

I dove into the new role as a Staff Analyst at Angi, another 2-sided marketplace for home services. But while I was working at Upwork and then Angi, I was spending my personal time building Career Compass, a mobile app that uses AI tools to help early career professionals navigate the workplace. This was an opportunity to learn all of the new AI tools and LLM engineering, learn to build products, try launching a full company as a single person, position for the new AI era and share what I've learned over the years with others. Like most learning, it has been incredibly rewarding and exciting.

Today I see myself as a hybrid analyst, data scientist, llm engineer that works across leading companies, partnering with Product, Finance and GTM functions, to lead organizations into the new AI era. I see the dawn of AI as a second machine age, like the industrial revolution, that will transform how we all work and life. And I want to work at the center of the transformation, building systems and solutions that deliver value and meaning to people.

My career path has certainly been a non-linear one and it is interesting that I started focused on AI, then took a long path through storytelling, artistry and non-technical work, and now I am working on AI again. But I think that is invaluable experience as we continue to build the new AI era. We should want artists and humanists also informing and contributing to this new era, not just engineers.

At this point, though I am only mid-way through my career, these are the three big takeways that I was share with early-career professionals:
1. Continual Learning and a Growth Mindset is the most important ingredient for building a rewarding, meaningful career that grows with you.
2. For almost all roles in an organization, doing the actual work accounts for about ~20% of your success, managing relationships, stakeholders and expectations is the much more important and impactful part of your job.
3. Take care of your health and mental well-being. If you are not well, then you can't do your best work. Also, none of us should live to work. Find real meaning and purpose in life outside of work; with your family, friends, and communities.
